## Goal for today
Train a ResNet-18 that looks at one Jenga block and outputs its category (flat / side / standing) and the 4 corners of its top face. We had no hardware, so all training pictures came from Blender.

## What we did
1. Made a new Python environment at `~/venvs/vision` (PyTorch 2.14, torchvision, OpenCV, matplotlib). The Mac's GPU works with PyTorch ("mps").
2. Downloaded 18 wood textures, 35 table textures, and 12 indoor lighting scenes from Poly Haven (CC0, 52 MB).
3. Wrote a Blender script that renders one block with a random pose, camera, wood, table, and lighting, and saves the exact top-corner positions. Rendered 4,000 pictures (about 35 minutes).
4. Checked the labels by eye. Every dot sits on a corner.
5. Fine-tuned ResNet-18 with two heads for 15 epochs (about 10 minutes). This is version 1.
6. Built the interactive page `media/jenga_net_explorer.html`, including a strip that shows the picture getting more abstract in each layer.
7. Real-photo test: downloaded 394 Creative Commons "Jenga" photos from Openverse. Only 6 showed loose blocks, so we made 21 crops and labeled their categories before running the network.
8. Version 1 struggled because real photos have many blocks. We made version 2: renders with up to 8 neighbor blocks, steeper camera (up to 60°), dark wood, and blur. Retrained for 20 epochs. (This session ran past midnight into Oct 25.)

## Results
- Worked: version 1 on 400 rendered test pictures: **98.3%** categories correct, **6.0 px** average corner miss, **7.8°** average angle miss.
- Worked: version 2 finds the middle block in crowded real photos. Corners graded by eye: **12 good / 9 rough / 0 wrong** (version 1: 7 / 12 / 2).
- Didn't work: categories on real photos. Version 1 got 8 of 18 right; version 2 got 5 of 18. Flat vs. side is hard to tell from steep angles, even for us.
- Didn't work: version 2 overfit (training loss 0.16 vs. validation loss 0.83). Its Blender-test scores dropped to 85.0% and 15.1 px.
- Not tested yet: photos of our own blocks from our own camera. Nothing ran on the real arm (no hardware today).

## Problems and fixes
| Problem | What caused it | How we fixed it |
|---|---|---|
| Poly Haven answered "403 Forbidden" | It blocks Python's default name tag (User-Agent) | The script introduces itself with its own User-Agent |
| A few textures had no color image | Some Poly Haven textures use different map names | Skip those and pick another |
| Blocks looked pinkish-white with no wood grain | Blender's default color transform (AgX) plus too much brightening | Switched to "Standard" colors; toned down the tint and the sun |
| Standing blocks were tiny in the picture | Camera too far away | Closer camera (16–32 cm) and more zoom |
| Labels overlapped on the loss chart | Both lines end at almost the same value | Push the end labels apart |
| Version 1 outlined the wrong block in real photos | It had only ever seen one block alone | Version 2 renders neighbor blocks; the label is always the middle one |
| Version 2 overfit | Crowded scenes are harder, and 3,200 training pictures weren't enough | Not fixed yet: render about 12,000 pictures |
| Real photos need credit | Creative Commons licenses require it | Credits on the web page and in `media/CREDITS.md` |

## Key idea (the 20% that mattered today)
Start from a network that already knows how to see (transfer learning), and make the training pictures in Blender, where the computer knows the exact answer for every picture. Then **test on real photos early**: a 98% score on fake pictures meant little until real photos showed what the fake ones were missing (neighbor blocks). Training pictures must look like the situation you'll really face.
