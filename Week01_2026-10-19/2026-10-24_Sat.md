## Goal for today
Train a program that looks at a picture of one Jenga block and tells us two things: how the block is lying (flat, on its side, or standing up) and where the 4 corners of its top face are. We had no hardware today, so all the training pictures were made in Blender, a free 3D program.

## What we did
1. Made a second Python setup at `~/venvs/vision` with PyTorch and other image tools. PyTorch can use the Mac's graphics chip.
2. Downloaded 18 wood textures, 35 table textures, and 12 indoor lighting scenes from Poly Haven. They're free to use (52 MB).
3. Wrote a Blender script that draws one block with a random position, camera angle, wood, table, and lighting, and saves the exact corner positions. It made 4,000 pictures in about 35 minutes.
4. Checked the corner labels by eye. Every dot was on a corner.
5. Trained version 1. We started from ResNet-18, a network that already knows how to see, and trained it for 15 rounds (about 10 minutes).
6. Built a web page, [`jenga_net_explorer.html`](media/jenga_net_explorer.html), that shows what the network sees at each layer.
7. Tested on real photos. We downloaded 394 free "Jenga" photos from Openverse. Only 6 showed loose blocks, so we cut out 21 blocks and wrote down the right answers by hand before running the network.
8. Version 1 struggled because real photos have many blocks. So we made version 2, trained on pictures with up to 8 neighbor blocks, steeper camera angles, dark wood, and blur. We trained it for 20 rounds. This ran past midnight.

## Results
- Worked: version 1 on 400 Blender test pictures. It was right about how the block lies 98.3% of the time. Corners were off by 6.0 pixels and the angle by 7.8° on average.
- Worked: version 2 finds the middle block in crowded real photos. We graded the corners by eye: 12 good, 9 rough, 0 wrong (version 1 got 7, 12, and 2).
- Didn't work: telling how the block lies in real photos. Version 1 got 8 of 18 right, and version 2 got 5 of 18. Flat and on-its-side are hard to tell apart from steep angles, even for us.
- Didn't work: version 2 overfit, meaning it memorized its training pictures instead of learning. Its Blender test scores dropped to 85.0% and 15.1 pixels.
- Not tested yet: photos of our own blocks from our own camera. There was no hardware today.

## Problems and fixes
| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Poly Haven refused our downloads ("403 Forbidden") | It blocks Python's default User-Agent, the name a program gives a website | The script sends its own User-Agent |
| A few textures had no color image | Some textures name their files differently | Skip those and pick another |
| Blocks looked pinkish-white with no wood grain | Blender's default color setting, plus too much brightening | Switched to "Standard" colors and toned down the tint and the sun |
| Standing blocks were tiny in the picture | The camera was too far away | Moved the camera closer (16 to 32 cm) and zoomed in |
| Labels overlapped on the training chart | Both lines end at almost the same value | Moved the end labels apart |
| Version 1 outlined the wrong block in real photos | It had only ever seen one block at a time | Version 2 adds neighbor blocks, and the answer is always the middle one |
| Version 2 overfit | Crowded scenes are harder, and 3,200 training pictures weren't enough | Not fixed yet. Plan: make about 12,000 pictures. |
| Real photos need credit | Creative Commons licenses require it | Credits are on the web page and in [`media/CREDITS.md`](media/CREDITS.md) |

## Key idea
Start from a network that already knows how to see, and make training pictures in Blender, where the computer knows the exact answer for every picture. Then test on real photos early. A 98% score on Blender pictures meant little until real photos showed what the Blender pictures were missing: other blocks nearby. Training pictures need to look like what the program will really see.
