## What we did
Times come from when each file was saved, so they're approximate.
1. **Morning, on the Mac: Jenga CNN version 3.** Version 3 now predicts all 8 corners of the block's 3D box, and then a math step called **PnP** (Perspective-n-Point) turns those corners into the block's real 3D position and rotation. PnP works because we know the block's exact size and the camera's lens. Later we added "PnP on the table": since the block lies on the table, only its spin is free, which made the rotation about 7× more accurate. Then we tested version 3 on the 21 real photo crops.
2. **Windows PC setup.** Set the PC's Ethernet port by hand to 192.168.1.100. The arm answered at 192.168.1.217 (xArm 6, firmware v2.5.1). Built the xArm Python environment at `~/venvs/xarm`, with a workaround for Windows' Smart App Control (see Problems).
3. **About 10:35: first D435 pictures.** `03_d435_snapshot.py` took one color picture and one depth picture. The depth picture gives a distance for every pixel.
4. **About 10:51: SmolVLA's first look.** `04_smolvla_first_look.py` gave the model our D435 photo, our arm's joint angles, and the instruction "Pick up the wooden block." It ran in Ubuntu (WSL, a Linux system inside Windows), because Windows blocks PyTorch. The arm did **not** move.
5. **About 11:27: planned our VLA project** (Lesson 5, plan): the first task will be "Pick up the wooden block and put it in the box," with demos recorded in teach mode. Wrote `05_teach_mode.py`.
6. **About 11:58: dropped the Wi-Fi plan.** School Wi-Fi doesn't work for teleop, so everything now connects by cable: a **Link cable** from the Quest Pro to the PC. Lesson 2 got an update note.
7. **About 12:00–13:02: built VR teleop** (`code/vr_teleop/`), went through versions 1 to 5, and tested on the real arm. Recorded 6 headset videos and 1 D435 video.
8. **About 13:09: pushed the whole notebook to GitHub** (github.com/Pigtot/Inventorx).
9. **About 13:17–13:23: one more real-arm teleop session**, recorded as `media/vr_2026-10-25_132338.mp4` (6 min 52 s). It still used the version 5 info panel.
10. **About 13:14–13:29: wrote a teleop upgrade with three new features** (Lesson 5 manual, sections E2, E3, and H):
    - **HUD (heads-up display)** in `hud.js`: an Iron Man-style overlay on the camera picture. Its main part is a spinning ring that marks **exactly where the gripper will touch down**, with the distance left (`LAND ↓ 42 mm`). It also shows a height ladder, how far open the gripper is, a turn compass, and X/Y/Z.
    - **Force feedback** (controller buzz): the arm has no force sensor, so the controllers *fake* the feeling of touch from what the program knows. A steady buzz means you're pushing against a safety limit. A buzz that grows stronger means the gripper is getting close to a surface (measured by the depth camera). A "clunk" means the gripper just closed on something.
    - **Camera calibration** in `calib.py`: the D435 is bolted *beside* the gripper, so the middle of the picture is not where the gripper lands. Calibration measures where the camera sits on the gripper. It's like learning where your eyes are compared to your fingertip: touch a dot, then look at it from several spots. The **Kabsch method** (a math recipe for lining up two sets of matching points) turns 4 or more of those looks into the camera's exact position and angle.
    - `vr_teleop.py` now also reads the depth picture (lined up with the color picture) and the gripper's real opening 5 times a second. That's how it can tell when the gripper is **holding** a block: it was told to close but stopped before closing all the way.

**Worked (on the computer only): camera calibration math**
- On made-up test data with realistic errors, `calib.py` found the camera's position within about 2 mm and its angle within about half a degree.

**Worked (Blender pictures only, not real photos): Jenga version 3**
- The **overfitting is mostly gone.** Overfitting means memorizing the practice pictures instead of learning. In version 2 the gap between training loss and validation loss was about 0.67. In version 3 it's about 0.09.

**Real photos (Jenga version 3):** 8 of 18 categories right. In 19 of 21 crops the 3D box lands on the middle block (graded by eye), but most boxes come out squashed flat.

## Problems and fixes
| Problem | What caused it | How we fixed it |
|---|---|---|
| Teleop v1: the arm kept moving after we let go | An arm error switched it out of teleop mode. In normal mode, every new target waits in a queue behind the old ones. | Version 2 and later use **mode 7**, where each new target *replaces* the last one. Letting go stops the arm at once, and the program quits on any arm error. |
| School Wi-Fi blocked the Quest from talking to the computer | School networks often block devices from talking to each other | Everything by cable. The Quest uses a Link cable to the PC. |
| The Link cable dropped every 1–4 minutes | Windows' USB power saving cuts the connection | Turned USB power saving off with `powercfg`. Not confirmed yet that this fixed it. |
| Smart App Control blocked the Python that `uv venv` made | That Python launcher isn't digitally signed | Built the environment with `python -m venv` instead |
| Smart App Control blocked PyTorch | 12 of PyTorch's files aren't signed | Run LeRobot and SmolVLA in Ubuntu (WSL) instead |
| Ubuntu (WSL) can't see USB cameras | WSL doesn't get USB devices by default | Split the work: Windows records (camera + arm), Ubuntu trains |
| The SDK printed warnings on every run | Its Blockly tools have old-style code | Ran `python -m compileall` on it once |
| `git push` couldn't ask for the GitHub login | Claude's command window can't show sign-in pop-ups | Ran the push in the Terminal panel and signed in through the browser |
| In VR you can't tell exactly where the gripper will land | The D435 is bolted beside the gripper, so the middle of the picture isn't the landing spot | Camera calibration (`calib.py`) plus the HUD's landing ring. Not tested on the real arm yet. |
| You can't feel when the gripper touches something | The arm has no force sensor | Controller buzz fakes it: at safety limits, near a surface (from the depth camera), and when the gripper closes on something. Not tested on the real arm yet. |

## Key idea (the 20% that mattered today)
**For live control, tell the arm "go here *instead*," not "go here *next*."** In normal mode the arm finishes every move in its queue, like a line at a cafeteria. When your hand sends 30 targets a second, that line grows, and the arm is still working through old targets after you've stopped. Mode 7 throws away the old target whenever a new one arrives, so the arm always chases your hand's *latest* position, and letting go really means stop. Behind that we stacked safety layers (workspace limits, the arm's own fence and speed cap, a dead-man timer, an on/off button) so one bug can't cause a crash.
