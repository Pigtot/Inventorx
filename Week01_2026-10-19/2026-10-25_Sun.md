## What we did
Times are approximate. They come from when files were saved.

1. **Morning, on the Mac: Jenga program version 3.** Version 3 finds all 8 corners of the block. Then a math step called PnP turns those corners into the block's real 3D position and angle. This works because we know the block's exact size. Next we used the fact that the block lies on the table, which made the angle about 7 times more accurate. Then we tested it on the 21 real photos from Saturday.
2. **Set up the Windows PC.** We set the PC's Ethernet port to 192.168.1.100, and the arm answered at 192.168.1.217. We made the Python setup at `~/venvs/xarm`. Windows' Smart App Control got in the way (see Problems).
3. **10:35 am: first camera pictures.** `03_d435_snapshot.py` saved a color picture and a depth picture. The depth picture shows how far away each pixel is.
4. **10:51 am: first try of SmolVLA.** `04_smolvla_first_look.py` gave the AI our camera photo, our arm's joint angles, and the instruction "Pick up the wooden block." It ran in Ubuntu (Linux inside Windows) because Windows blocks PyTorch. The arm did not move.
5. **11:27 am: planned the AI project** ([Lesson 5, plan](Lesson05_VLA_Plan_Without_VR.md)). The first task will be "Pick up the wooden block and put it in the box." We'll record examples by moving the arm by hand (teach mode). We wrote `05_teach_mode.py`.
6. **11:58 am: dropped the Wi-Fi plan.** School Wi-Fi doesn't work for VR control, so the Quest Pro now connects to the PC with a Link cable. We added a note to Lesson 2.
7. **12:00 to 1:02 pm: built VR control** (`code/vr_teleop/`). We went through versions 1 to 5 and tested them on the real arm. We recorded 6 headset videos and 1 camera video.
8. **1:09 pm: pushed the notebook to GitHub.**
9. **1:17 to 1:23 pm: one more session on the real arm**, using version 5. It's recorded in `vr_2026-10-25_132338.mp4` (6 min 52 s).
10. **1:14 to 1:29 pm: wrote three new VR features.** None of them have been tried on the real arm yet. The [VR control manual](Lesson05_VR_Teleop_Manual.md) explains them in sections E2, E3, and H.
    - **HUD:** information drawn on the camera picture. A spinning ring shows where the gripper will touch down and how far away that is. It also shows height, how open the gripper is, and the X/Y/Z position.
    - **Controller buzz:** the arm can't feel force, so the controllers buzz to fake it. A steady buzz means you're at a safety limit. A buzz that grows stronger means the gripper is getting close to a surface. A "clunk" means the gripper just closed on something.
    - **Camera calibration** (`calib.py`): the camera is bolted beside the gripper, so the center of the picture isn't where the gripper lands. You touch a dot with the gripper, then click the dot in the picture from 4 or more spots. The program works out exactly where the camera sits.
    - `vr_teleop.py` now also reads the depth picture and how open the gripper really is, 5 times a second. That lets it tell when the gripper is holding a block: it was told to close, but stopped partway.

## Results
**Worked on the real hardware:**
- `01_hello_arm.py` read the arm's joint angles from the Windows PC.
- `03_d435_snapshot.py` saved a color picture and a depth picture from the camera.
- The gripper reported its position: 840 out of 850, almost fully open.
- Ubuntu (inside Windows) can reach the arm over the network.
- VR control version 4 moved the real arm smoothly with no errors. Twisting your hand turned the gripper.
- VR control version 5 also works on the real arm. The A button turns motion on and off, and the 3D model of the arm on screen follows the real one.
- The 1:23 pm session was on the real arm. At 3:26 in the video, the panel shows live joint angles, which only appear when the real arm is connected. Motion is on, the gripper is set to closed, and Jenga blocks are in view.
- Recording from Chrome saved the headset and camera videos straight into `media/`.

**Worked on the computer only:**
- SmolVLA runs on the PC's graphics card (RTX 5070 Ti). But its answers are for the small hobby arm it was trained on (SO-100), so they mean nothing for our arm. We never sent them to the arm.
- Camera calibration math: on made-up test data, it found the camera's position within about 2 mm and its angle within about half a degree.
- Jenga version 3 on 1,200 Blender pictures: it was right about how the block lies 94.4% of the time (version 2: 85.0%). Its 3D position was off by about 1 cm and its angle by 3.7°. It also overfits much less than version 2.

**Real photos, Jenga version 3:** it was right about how the block lies in 8 of 18 photos. In 19 of 21 photos its 3D box lands on the right block, but most boxes come out squashed flat.

**Didn't work:**
- VR control version 1. The arm hit an error that switched it out of live-control mode. After that, moves piled up in a line, so the arm lagged and kept moving after we let go. Version 2 fixed this.
- School Wi-Fi, and Air Link (the Quest's wireless connection to the PC).
- The Link cable dropped every 1 to 4 minutes while Windows' USB power saving was on.
- PyTorch on Windows, because Smart App Control blocks it.

**Not tested yet:**
- `02_first_move.py` and `05_teach_mode.py` on the real arm
- Picking up a Jenga block with VR control. In the 1:23 pm video the gripper is set to closed above the blocks, but you can't tell if it picked one up.
- The new HUD, controller buzz, and camera calibration on the real arm. No calibration has been saved yet.
- Whether turning off USB power saving stopped the Link cable drops

## Problems and fixes
| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| VR version 1 kept moving after we let go | An arm error switched it out of live-control mode, so moves waited in a line | Version 2 and later use mode 7, where each new target replaces the old one. Letting go stops the arm, and the program quits on any arm error. |
| School Wi-Fi blocked the Quest from reaching the PC | School networks often block devices from talking to each other | Connect everything by cable. The Quest uses a Link cable. |
| The Link cable dropped every 1 to 4 minutes | Windows' USB power saving | Turned it off with `powercfg`. Not checked yet if this fixed it. |
| Smart App Control blocked the Python made by `uv venv` | That Python launcher isn't digitally signed | Used `python -m venv` instead |
| Smart App Control blocked PyTorch | 12 PyTorch files aren't digitally signed | Run SmolVLA in Ubuntu (WSL) instead |
| Ubuntu can't see USB cameras | WSL doesn't get USB devices by default | Windows records the camera and arm, Ubuntu trains |
| The arm library printed warnings on every run | Old code in its Blockly tools | Ran `python -m compileall` on it once |
| `git push` couldn't ask for the GitHub login | Claude's command window can't show sign-in pop-ups | Ran the push in the Terminal panel |
| In VR you can't tell where the gripper will land | The camera is beside the gripper, not in the middle | Camera calibration and the HUD's landing ring. Not tried on the real arm yet. |
| You can't feel when the gripper touches something | The arm has no force sensor | Controller buzz. Not tried on the real arm yet. |

## Key idea
For live control, tell the arm "go here instead," not "go here next." In its normal mode, the arm finishes every move in its line, like a line at a cafeteria. The headset sends 30 targets a second, so the line grows, and the arm is still working through old targets after you stop. Mode 7 throws away the old target when a new one arrives, so the arm always follows where your hand is now, and letting go means stop. We also added backup safety layers so one bug can't cause a crash: a box the arm must stay inside, a speed limit, a timer that stops the arm if the headset goes quiet, and an on/off button.
